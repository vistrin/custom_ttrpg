Design Themes — Living Document

Compiled from prior mechanics/worldbuilding sessions + today's brain-dump. Update freely — this is meant to be scribbled on.

## Tone & Philosophy (locked north star)
- "Action archeologist," not mercenary. The reason to delve into ruins is curiosity and understanding, not loot-and-run.
- Wonder over nihilism. Explicit rejection of "humans always destroy what they build," "power always corrupts," and grimdark moral ambiguity as a default mode. Consequences should exist without the world hating the players.
- Fantasy as a relief valve. You like fantasy specifically because it lets you hand-wave practical/logistical details you'd otherwise get stuck on as GM — freedom over simulation.
- "Post post-apocalyptic." The fall is ancient history, more myth than memory. Nobody alive carries guilt for it; societies have had time to actually succeed and stabilize.
- "Evil is real" — stated flatly, not for shock value. Not moral relativism, and not a metaphysical force validating a divine order — a plain, matter-of-fact claim about how choices land. The working test: absent mutual consent, only a clear, present, imminent life-threatening circumstance justifies taking someone's right to choose. Anything else is a violation, and it doesn't feel good to anyone — including the person doing it — regardless of how they rationalize it. The story doesn't need to convince anyone a wrong happened; it just did. This can stay purely a claim about lived/felt experience (like "betrayal hurts") rather than a cosmic judgment, keeping it consistent with the no-validated-gods rule below. Villains can rationalize all they want — the narrative never has to validate the rationalization.
- Demons, definitely. They don't need to be metaphysical Evil incarnate (that reopens the architect problem) — they can just be beings whose nature runs on taking choice away without the imminent-threat justification, the way a shark just hunts. Best played matter-of-fact/banal rather than mustache-twirling — corrupting a Working or possessing without asking not out of malice but because "asking" isn't a concept that occurs to them. Where they ultimately come from can stay unconfirmed, same as everything else in the cosmology. Good candidate source for darker Overreach/Catastrophic content and possibly a flavor of undeath.
- "The Devil didn't make you do it — you let him." No externalized blame, ever. A demon can offer, tempt, bargain, corrupt — but the choice to accept stays the mortal's. This is the demon corollary to the consent-based definition of evil above: it protects the "everyone already knows it was wrong" clause from being escaped via "I was possessed/tricked/overpowered." Keeps agency (and therefore consequence) squarely with the person who said yes.
- Working idea: demons have Will but no Way. They can generate raw magical force but have no method to shape, aim, or express it — no Place, Tools, Form, Cost, or Company of their own. This is why they want mortals: a mortal supplies the Way (the mechanism), and in exchange the demon supplies/takes on the Will (the raw power) instead of the caster paying that toll themselves. Interesting because it reframes a demonic bargain as a literal mechanical trade within the existing Will/Way split, not a separate magic system — and it gives a concrete answer to "why deal with a demon at all": you get to cast beyond your own Will capacity, at the cost of the strings a demon attaches to lending it. Open question: does the demon still take the toll (strain/wounds/Overreach risk) on the mortal's behalf, redirect it, or does accepting a demon's Will bypass the toll entirely and replace it with a different kind of cost (the bargain itself)? That last option seems the most interesting and most on-theme (cost/benefit without cosmic balance still applies — the cost just isn't paid to the universe, it's paid to the demon).
- George R.R. Martin as a negative influence. World and characters are compelling, but the storytelling itself reads as patriarchal, juvenile, and a little pointless. Useful as a specific counter-example alongside the Forgotten Realms one below — not "grimdark is bad" in the abstract, but a concrete gut-check for tone when a scene or plot thread starts drifting that direction.

## Magic — Cosmology & Feel
- Mystical, not "poorly understood." The unease comes from knowing how little is known — not from the world being under-explained by you as designer. Forgotten Realms is the named negative example (magic with fully certain, documented cosmology).
- No confirmed cosmology, ever. Different cultures/traditions offer different explanatory frameworks (gods, spirits, inner will, wizardly hypothesis) — none validated, none ever will be. This is permanent, not a mystery box to eventually open.
- No validated gods / intelligent design. Explicitly bored of this trope. Religious institutions can believe in gods and structure practice around that belief — but the setting itself never confirms they're real or responsible for how magic works.
- A little Warhammer 40k — specifically the Warp vibe. Not the grimdark/satire tone, just the sense of a parallel reality that's chaotic, alive, and actively dangerous to perceive or touch too directly. Worth revisiting when The Will/backfire/Overreach mechanics get their next narrative pass — there may be a "place you reach into" quality worth borrowing for how a Working feels from the inside, separate from its mechanical risk.
- Earthdawn as a structural touchstone. Post-apocalyptic, world-as-unknown-place framing — reinforces the "verification is the adventuring premise" idea already established, and is a good reference for how a setting can be post-collapse without being grim about it.
- Naming describes method, not metaphysics. Skill/tradition names must never imply a cosmological claim is objectively true.
- Dragons know something and aren't telling. Standing narrative hook — a source of unverified insight, consistent with the lore/legend framework below.
- Magic is democratized but hard. Everyone does small, half-realized magic (folk charms, half-remembered blessings) without recognizing it as such — magic isn't a separate genre element, it's woven into how belief/intention already work at every skill level.
- Wizards are engineers, not scientists. They don't research universal laws — they solve specific, personal engineering problems and keep a build log of what worked. A spellbook isn't a textbook (which would make two wizards' books redundant, just confirming the same facts) — it's a lab notebook of hard-won working solutions, failed attempts included, which is why finding a rival's spellbook is exciting rather than duplicate material, and why stealing one is stealing a proven blueprint, not forbidden knowledge. Explains the Necronomicon aesthetic (cramped margins, crossed-out failures, blood instead of ink) as an authentic build log rather than "unscientific" set dressing. Also explains wizardly precision in casting: not piety or caution-as-virtue, but an engineer's respect for tolerances — you don't overbuild past spec, because both waste and shortfall get someone hurt. Sits opposite the cleric/Way archetype's relationship to tools and preparation: a wizard's tool is chosen and built for precision (works the same in anyone's hands); a Way caster's tool is earned through years of lived use (works worse divorced from the person who shaped it).
- No mana-as-battery. Not a fan of the "recharge a mystical battery every day" model — magic shouldn't feel gated by a resource pool you top off on a timer. Consistent with the existing toll structure (strain/wounds paid per-cast, not drawn from a reserve) but worth stating explicitly as a thing to keep watching for, especially if downtime/rest mechanics get built out later.
- Cost/benefit, not cosmic balance. Casting should have real, felt cost — but never framed as the universe "keeping the books," correcting an imbalance, or sending a bill for what you spent. The risk in The Will/Overreach comes from the act being difficult and dangerous in itself (a stretch beyond what a person can reliably hold), not from some equilibrium-tracking force punishing you for taking out a loan. No karmic ledger, no "magic always demands a price" mysticism — just: this is hard, hard things go wrong, and going wrong has real consequences.
- Underlying throughline: hubris without karma. Catastrophe comes from exceeding a limit, at every scale — the Event (civilization-scale Working overreaching), Push/Overreach (individual-scale version, deliberately built to rhyme with the Event), vampires/liches/mummies (people who exceeded the limit death imposes), demons (beings for whom "there is a limit" doesn't register at all). No one is being punished for pride — reality is just unforgiving about scope. Closer to physically overloading a system than to gods smiting the proud. Likely to keep resurfacing across sessions; worth watching for.

## Magic — Four-Mode Exploration (early-stage, not locked)
An old idea resurfacing: a possible four-axis framing of how a caster relates to "the universe," read through the consent lens that's emerged elsewhere in the project. Distinct from — and not yet reconciled with — the locked Will/Way system; this is exploratory and sits alongside it for now.

- Command (≈ Will): I take. Consent isn't sought. Precise, costly, self-authored outcome.
- Encourage (≈ Way): I persuade, cooperate, work with. Consent is cultivated.
- Ask (unnamed): I request. Consent is explicitly sought and can be refused. Bounded favor, not a guaranteed outcome.
- Surrender (unnamed, "wild magic"): I stop directing and let an indifferent, unauthored force move through the gap I leave — not "something acts through me" (which smuggles in an agent/intent, at odds with the no-validated-cosmology rule), but closer to unclenching a fist in a current. Trades precision for scale: reached for when the desired outcome exceeds what a controlled, shaped Working could ever produce. Symmetrical opposite of Command (imposing shape vs. withdrawing shape).

Covers a wider design space than it first appears, once framed as "precision traded for scale" rather than a fixed spell list: beyond-knowledge (divination, prophecy), beyond-reach (planar travel, teleportation), beyond-power-ceiling (Hail-Mary transformations, channeling more raw force than one's own Will could produce), beyond-choice-of-who-answers (summoning — opening a door without commanding what walks through, a possible mechanical link to the demon Will/Way trade idea), beyond-temporal-reach (curses/blessings meant to outlast the caster).

Genre-recognizable as "wild magic" — powerful, uncontrolled, chaotic — which gives players an intuitive read on the trade-off even before the deeper logic is explained.

Rejected fourth candidate — "Trick the universe": never felt right, and the consent lens explains why. Tricking isn't a fourth posture toward the universe — it's a corrupted version of Ask, getting compliance while deliberately not hearing a real answer, dressed up to look like a request. Same structural move as the necromancer (technically same mechanic, but refusing to listen). Not a discipline in its own right, so it doesn't belong in the lineup.

## The Event & Knowledge Transmission
- Three-era timeline: Old Age → The Event → New Age.
- The Event is a permanent, intentional mystery — not a withheld answer waiting to be revealed later. Leading (non-canon) theory: a civilization-scale magical Working that failed from a timing/coordination error — deliberately rhyming the world's founding trauma with the magic system's own individual risks.
- "Heard of, never verified" knowledge model (the New Yorker-who's-heard-of-London idea). Old Age names, cultures, and artifacts persist as fragments; nothing can be confirmed. Verification itself is the adventuring premise.
- Lore vs. Legend distinction: Lore = agreed common knowledge. Legend = a claim tied to a specific, traceable, potentially unreliable source. Flagged as both a worldbuilding tool and a possible player-facing sourcing/reliability mechanic — good content lever for artifacts and ruins.

## Species / Ancestry (new thread — not yet built out mechanically)
- Differences between peoples (elves, dwarves, humans, tieflings, etc.) should be mechanically real and interesting, not just cosmetic culture-paint.
- But you're done with "ancient grudges" as a reason for prejudice. Mechanical difference should not be used as in-world justification for racism/hatred between peoples. Differences = flavor and gameplay variety, not a built-in excuse for bigotry.
- Open question for later: what replaces "ancient grudges" as the social texture between peoples, if anything needs to?

## Death, Resurrection & Undeath
- The universe has an "opinion" about death — resurrection is mechanically possible but something it visibly, consistently resists.
- Resurrection sickness: mechanically recoverable, narratively permanent. The returned are never unchanged — memory loss, detachment, hallucinations, obsession, violent outbursts, difficulty reconnecting are established texture. This is a trauma profile, not a curse-effect or stat penalty.
- "Way changes odds, Push changes stakes — nothing buys the stakes down." Even a master ritualist going Desperate faces the same raw catastrophic risk as a first-session farmer. Mastery narrows how likely, never how bad. This directly supports resurrection staying dangerous forever, for everyone, regardless of skill.
- New thread: Undeath as a taxonomy of how someone refused the universe's answer, rather than separate monster types:
  - Vampires, liches, mummies — people who took active, deliberate steps to "keep going" past their limit; cheating-death-through-effort.
  - Ghosts, wights, ghouls — people who never took action, just refused to "let it go"; unresolved anger/attachment rather than a Working.
- This reframes undeath as a spectrum of relationships to death (active defiance vs. passive refusal) rather than a flavor-text monster manual. Fits cleanly with the existing Overreach/Catastrophic-results framework and the "permanent results resist conventional treatment" rule already locked for magic backfires.

### 2×2 grid — whose will, and won't vs. can't
|  | Self | Other |
|---|---|---|
| **Won't** | Liches, vampires, mummies — active defiance | (open — see revenant idea below) |
| **Can't** | Ghosts, wraiths, wights, ghouls — passive refusal | Zombies, skeletons — forced/compelled reanimation |

Top row = agency (choosing to defy or refuse). Bottom row = lack of agency (compulsion, no choice involved).

- Working idea — what makes a zombie a zombie (good intent vs. necromancer): Both cases run the same Working, and the dead person gets the same moment of consent to choose whether to return. The difference isn't the mechanic — it's whether the caster cares about the answer.
  - Well-meaning but ignorant: the dead person declines, but a grief-blinded or untrained caster doesn't recognize/respect the refusal as final — the body still moves on leftover momentum from a Working that got its answer and pushed through anyway. Mindless husk, not malice. The lesson is "don't let grief cloud your judgment," not "you're evil."
  - The necromancer: identical Working, identical refusal — except he was never listening for one. "Stay or go, I don't care, I just need your body." Not failing to notice consent was declined, like the well-meaning caster — consent was never a factor in his goal at all.
- This sharpens the consent rule one level further: the operative question isn't "did you get consent" but "did you care whether you got it." Same outcome (an animated corpse), same Working, radically different moral weight — a direct escalation of the "the magic doesn't care, the world does" principle already locked under Cost/Offering.
- Dark implication, free of charge: a zombie horde isn't people who couldn't say no. Every one of them did. Nobody was listening.
- Open wrinkle for later: does a declined return always produce a mindless husk, or does refusal sometimes mean the body just stays dead — with the husk outcome specifically reserved for a Working that partially succeeds despite the refusal?
- What a "correct" resurrection looks like: the same throughline, run with full integrity. Consent isn't just sought (as with the well-meaning failure above) or heard — it's informed. The returned person understands exactly what they're agreeing to: resurrection sickness is narratively permanent, the road back is hard, and coming back is inherently counter-intuitive and doesn't feel right, full stop, no exceptions. "The King might return if the cause is righteous enough, but he knows the rest of his story is likely not a happy one."
  - Critically, it isn't the caster's reasons that need to be righteous — that would just reintroduce the universe grading sincerity, which is already rejected. It's that the dead person is the one who has to decide it's worth it, weighing the cost against whatever cause is being asked of them.
  - This preserves "no cosmic balance" cleanly: the universe isn't judging the reason as good or bad, it's just always true that resurrection is unforgiving and effortful — same physics-not-punishment framing already locked for Overreach. A righteous cause doesn't make the Working easier or the sickness lighter.
  - Reframes resurrection sickness as an honest, known toll rather than a punishment: the King comes back aware he's buying borrowed time and a harder road, choosing that trade with open eyes — not tricked into returning, and not spared the cost for having a good reason.
- Working idea — "someone else won't let you go" (revenant?): Not compulsion for someone else's purposes (that's the zombie/skeleton case) — a living person's refusal to accept the loss, weaponized into a Working that holds the dead at the threshold. Ties directly to the existing Tether action. The dead person never consented to being held here, and there's no imminent-threat justification — so under the "evil is real" definition, this can be a genuinely loving, well-intentioned act that is still a violation. Probably the most tragic category: unlike a lich (self-serving) or zombie (obviously monstrous), this is a grieving parent/spouse/friend refusing to say goodbye, and the story doesn't need to villainize them for it to still be honest that it's wrong. The undead's own state might reflect the borrowed agency — aware, but tethered, unable to act fully on their own will because the thing sustaining them isn't their choice at all.

## Mechanics — System Feel
- Genesys's dual-axis resolution (success/failure × advantage/threat) is a core draw. Rolls should rarely resolve to nothing happening — failure with a silver lining, or success with a complication, keeps play dynamic. This is a stronger pull for you than D&D's binary pass/fail, even though d20 itself is "fine."
- Accessible on-ramp, real depth underneath. New players shouldn't face "limitless options" paralysis, but the system should reward players who get curious and start combining mechanics creatively — depth as something you grow into, not something thrown at you on character creation.
- Design validation by analogy. You like checking new mechanics against existing systems you trust (Force and Destiny's power tree for branching upgrades) rather than pure theorycrafting from scratch.

## Working Conventions
- Two parallel tracks: a mechanics conversation and a worldbuilding/narrative conversation — themes should stay consistent across both, but sessions are kept separate.
- Locked decisions get flagged explicitly and stay settled; open questions are tracked and deliberately deferred rather than resolved on the spot.
- Versioned reference docs at milestones — new versions get new names rather than overwriting prior ones.

- Sacrifice-of-other vs. sacrifice-of-self Offering, mechanically
  - Working answer: the Offering measures objective magnitude of what was spent, not the caster's sentimental attachment to it — a life is a life mechanically, whether mourned or shrugged off. This avoids reintroducing cosmic-balance-by-another-name (the universe grading sincerity). Self-sacrifice is always clean — you can't violate your own consent. Sacrifice-of-other splits cleanly on the existing consent rule: a willing offering is fine; a non-consensual one is a straightforward violation under the "evil is real" definition already locked — "they meant nothing to me" isn't a mitigating factor, it's the reason it's evil. The magic works either way; the magic just doesn't care, which is the point.
